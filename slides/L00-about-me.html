What an actuary does

Insurers sell a promise: if something bad happens, we pay. Someone has to decide the price of that promise.

An actuary prices it with probability. How often does a car crash? How large is a typical claim? How much money must be set aside so the company survives a terrible year?

That is the content of this course, with the insurance removed: probability first, then statistics for estimating it from data.

What I researched: care for the elderly

The data came from nursing homes in the canton of Geneva: more than 21,000 residents, followed from 1996 to 2018.

Three questions an insurer or a nursing home has to answer:

  • How long will a resident stay?
  • How much care will they need while they are there?
  • What will it cost?

All three are probability questions with real money behind them.

Three papers, three methods

Written with Joël Wagner, University of Lausanne.

Duration and intensity of care (Annals of Actuarial Science, 2023, with M. Bladt and M. Fuino). An accelerated failure time model for how long people stay, and beta regression for how intensive the care is.

Health profiles (Insurance: Mathematics and Economics, 2024). Spectral clustering finds eight typical profiles of residents. Multinomial logistic regression shows what decides the profile.

Costs over time (Insurance: Mathematics and Economics, 2025). A multi-state Markov model: a resident moves between four care levels, from nearly independent to severely dependent, and each level has a price.

You will meet the same ideas here

The Markov model is conditional probability, repeated: given that a resident is in state 2 today, what is the chance of state 3 tomorrow? That is Lecture 2.

Length of stay is a random variable with a distribution, an expectation and a variance. That is Lectures 3 to 5.

Regression and estimation from data come in module 3.

Why this matters for a business student

Pricing, credit risk, inventory, quality control, A/B tests, surveys. All of them ask the same thing: how sure can we be, given what we know?

A bank has just approved 1,000 loans. Some borrowers will not pay back, and nobody knows which ones.

Module 2: before any data. If each loan defaults with probability p, how many defaults should the bank plan for, and how far from that number can the real count be?

Module 3: after six months. The bank now has real data. How well does it know p, and when is a change in the default rate more than bad luck?
