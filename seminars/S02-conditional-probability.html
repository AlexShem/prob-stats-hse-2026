Problem 1

Short drill, if time allows.

HR at an IT outsourcing firm in Nizhny Novgorod asked all 504 employees two questions: do you work mostly from home or from the office, and are you satisfied with your job? Four people did not answer and are left out. The other 500 answers are in the table.

Satisfied Not satisfied Total
Remote 168 42 210
Office 174 116 290
Total 342 158 500

One of the 500 is picked at random. Let \(S\) be “satisfied” and \(R\) “works remotely”.

  1. Find \(\Prob(S\mid R)\) and \(\Prob(R\mid S)\).
  2. In one or two sentences of words, without formulas: why are the two numbers different?
  3. Are \(S\) and \(R\) independent? One word, then the number that shows it.
NoteSolution

  1. Every employee is equally likely, so Definition 2.1 becomes a ratio of counts: \[ \Prob(S\mid R)=\frac{168}{210}=0.80,\qquad \Prob(R\mid S)=\frac{168}{342}\approx 0.491 . \]

  2. The numerator is the same, 168 people, but the group we look inside is not. The first number asks what share of remote workers are satisfied. The second asks what share of satisfied people work remotely, and since most of the firm sits in the office, the satisfied group is split almost half and half even though remote workers are happier. It is the swap from Example 2.1.

  3. No. \(\Prob(S)=342/500=0.684\), while \(\Prob(S\mid R)=0.80\). For independent events these two would be equal (Definition 2.2). In the office the share is \(174/290=0.60\).

Two warnings. The table does not say that working from home makes people happier. It could go the other way: managers may let their most satisfied people go remote. And whether a gap like 0.80 against 0.60 in one survey could be chance is a question for a test we meet in module 3.

import pandas as pd

survey = pd.DataFrame({"satisfied": [168, 174], "not satisfied": [42, 116]},
                      index=["remote", "office"])
n_total = survey.values.sum()
both = survey.loc["remote", "satisfied"]
print(f"P(S | R) = {both / survey.loc['remote'].sum():.3f}")
print(f"P(R | S) = {both / survey['satisfied'].sum():.3f}")
print(f"P(S)     = {survey['satisfied'].sum() / n_total:.3f}")
P(S | R) = 0.800
P(R | S) = 0.491
P(S)     = 0.684

Problem 2

Zarina is at the New Year party of a logistics company in Tyumen. The raffle box holds 40 tickets. Four of them win something: a weekend in Sochi, two pairs of headphones and a cake. She draws 3 tickets, one after another, and does not put any back.

  1. What is the probability that she wins nothing?
  2. What is the probability that she wins exactly one prize?

Solve both with a probability tree (you only need to draw the branches you use). Then check both answers by counting, as in Lecture 1.

NoteSolution

Let \(W_i\) be “ticket \(i\) wins” and \(N_i\) “ticket \(i\) does not win”.

  1. The path \(N_1N_2N_3\). After each losing ticket there is one ticket fewer in the box and one losing ticket fewer. By Equation 2.1, \[ \Prob(N_1\cap N_2\cap N_3)=\frac{36}{40}\cdot\frac{35}{39}\cdot\frac{34}{38}=\frac{42\,840}{59\,280}=\frac{357}{494}\approx 0.723 . \]

  2. Three paths end with exactly one prize: \(W_1N_2N_3\), \(N_1W_2N_3\) and \(N_1N_2W_3\). For example, \[ \Prob(N_1\cap W_2\cap N_3)=\frac{36}{40}\cdot\frac{4}{39}\cdot\frac{35}{38}=\frac{5040}{59\,280}. \] The other two paths have the same numbers in a different order, so the same product. The paths are mutually exclusive, and \[ \Prob(\text{exactly one})=3\cdot\frac{5040}{59\,280}=\frac{15\,120}{59\,280}=\frac{63}{247}\approx 0.255 . \]

Check by counting, exactly as in Example 2.3. Order does not matter, so the outcomes are the \(\binom{40}{3}=9880\) sets of three tickets (Definition 1.8). No prize: \(\binom{36}{3}=7140\) sets, and \(7140/9880=357/494\). Exactly one prize: \(\binom41\binom{36}{2}=4\cdot630=2520\) sets, and \(2520/9880=63/247\). Same answers.

The usual mistake is to use \(4/40\) at every step, as if tickets went back into the box. That gives \(3\cdot0.1\cdot0.9^2=0.243\) for (b), close but wrong.

from math import comb

p_none = (36 / 40) * (35 / 39) * (34 / 38)            # chain rule
p_one = 3 * (36 / 40) * (4 / 39) * (35 / 38)          # three paths
count_none = comb(36, 3) / comb(40, 3)
count_one = comb(4, 1) * comb(36, 2) / comb(40, 3)
print(f"P(no prize)          = {p_none:.3f}, by counting {count_none:.3f}")
print(f"P(exactly one prize) = {p_one:.3f}, by counting {count_one:.3f}")
P(no prize)          = 0.723, by counting 0.723
P(exactly one prize) = 0.255, by counting 0.255

Problem 3

Short drill, if time allows.

A furniture delivery firm in Samara keeps a record of two kinds of trouble with an order: the sofa or wardrobe arrives damaged, or it arrives late. Let \(D\) be “the item arrives damaged”, usually a scratch picked up while loading the van, and \(L\) “the delivery is late”. Last month’s records give \[ \Prob(D)=0.10,\qquad \Prob(L)=0.20,\qquad \Prob(D\cap L)=0.02 . \]

  1. Are \(D\) and \(L\) independent? Find \(\Prob(L\mid D)\) as well.
  2. Find the probability that an order had at least one of the two problems, and the probability that it had neither.
  3. Optional, if time allows. At a second firm, the driver checks every item at the customer’s door. A damaged item is not handed over: the delivery is cancelled and a new item is sent later as a new order, so a delivery with a damaged item is never recorded as late. Again \(\Prob(D)=0.10\) and \(\Prob(L)=0.20\). Answer (a) and (b) for this firm and say what has changed.
NoteSolution

  1. \(\Prob(D)\Prob(L)=0.10\cdot0.20=0.02=\Prob(D\cap L)\), so \(D\) and \(L\) are independent by Definition 2.2. In conditional form, \(\Prob(L\mid D)=0.02/0.10=0.20=\Prob(L)\): learning that the item was damaged does not change the chance that the delivery was late. That fits the story, because scratches come from loading in the warehouse and delays come from traffic.

  2. At least one: \(\Prob(D\cup L)=0.10+0.20-0.02=0.28\) (Theorem 1.2). Neither: by Theorem 2.3, \(D^c\) and \(L^c\) are independent too, so \(\Prob(D^c\cap L^c)=0.90\cdot0.80=0.72\), which is \(1-0.28\) as it should be.

  3. Now \(D\) and \(L\) are mutually exclusive, \(\Prob(D\cap L)=0\). That is not \(0.02\), so they are not independent. \(\Prob(L\mid D)=0\): once you know the item was damaged, you know the delivery was not recorded as late. At least one: \(0.10+0.20=0.30\). Neither: \(1-0.30=0.70\). The product \(0.90\cdot0.80=0.72\) is wrong here, because Theorem 2.3 needs independence.

p_damaged, p_late = 0.10, 0.20
for firm, p_both in [("firm 1", 0.02), ("firm 2", 0.00)]:
    p_either = p_damaged + p_late - p_both
    print(f"{firm}: P(L | D) = {p_both / p_damaged:.2f}, "
          f"P(at least one) = {p_either:.2f}, P(neither) = {1 - p_either:.2f}")
firm 1: P(L | D) = 0.20, P(at least one) = 0.28, P(neither) = 0.72
firm 2: P(L | D) = 0.00, P(at least one) = 0.30, P(neither) = 0.70

Problem 4

Short drill, if time allows.

Find the mistakes. The problem was:

A cinema chain in Tver sells its tickets through a website that runs on two servers in two different data centres. On a given day each server fails with probability 0.05. The site goes down only if both servers fail. What is the probability that the site goes down on a given day?

Gleb from the IT department wrote this solution.

Let \(A\) be “server 1 fails” and \(B\) “server 2 fails”. The site goes down when \(A\cap B\) happens. By the definition of conditional probability, \(\Prob(A\cap B)=\Prob(A\mid B)/\Prob(B)\). Server 1 fails with probability 0.05 whatever server 2 does, so \(\Prob(A\mid B)=0.05\). Then \(\Prob(A\cap B)=0.05/0.05=1\). The site goes down every day, and the second server does not help at all.

  1. There are two mistakes, one in a formula and one in what Gleb does not say. Find both and give the correct answer.
  2. To save money, the chain moves both servers into one data centre with a single power supply. Does the probability that the site goes down go up, go down or stay the same? No number needed.
NoteSolution
  1. The formula. Definition 2.1 says \(\Prob(A\mid B)=\Prob(A\cap B)/\Prob(B)\). Multiplying both sides by \(\Prob(B)\) gives \(\Prob(A\cap B)=\Prob(A\mid B)\,\Prob(B)\), which is Theorem 2.2. Gleb divided by \(\Prob(B)\) where he should have multiplied by it. A quick check shows that something is wrong: \(A\cap B\) is inside \(A\), so \(\Prob(A\cap B)\le\Prob(A)=0.05\) by Theorem 1.2. An answer of 1 cannot be right.

What is not said. “Server 1 fails with probability 0.05 whatever server 2 does” means \(\Prob(A\mid B)=\Prob(A)\), which is the assumption that \(A\) and \(B\) are independent (Definition 2.2). The assumption is reasonable here, since the servers sit in different data centres, but it is an assumption. A solution has to say it, and say why it is believable.

Correct answer, assuming independence: \[ \Prob(A\cap B)=0.05\cdot0.05=0.0025, \] about one day in 400. The simulation below gives the same number, up to random variation.

  1. It goes up. With one power supply, a power cut takes down both servers at once. Then learning that server 2 failed makes it more likely that server 1 failed too, so \(\Prob(A\mid B)>0.05\) and \(\Prob(A\cap B)=\Prob(A\mid B)\Prob(B)>0.0025\). If, say, half of server 2’s failures were power cuts, \(\Prob(A\mid B)\) would be at least 0.5 and the site would go down at least ten times as often.
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
n_days, p_fail = 1_000_000, 0.05
server1_down = rng.random(n_days) < p_fail
server2_down = rng.random(n_days) < p_fail  # drawn separately: independent
p_site_down = np.mean(server1_down & server2_down)
print(f"simulated P(site down) = {p_site_down:.4f}, formula = {p_fail * p_fail:.4f}")
simulated P(site down) = 0.0024, formula = 0.0025

Problem 5

Timur buys online ads for a flower delivery service in Moscow. From a year of data he knows that 30% of the clicks from one ad channel turn into an order. Treat clicks from different people as mutually independent. On the Monday before 8 March, the first six clicks of the day all turn into orders.

  1. Is the seventh click more likely than 30% to turn into an order? One word, then one line.
  2. On Sunday evening, what was the probability that the first six clicks on Monday would all convert?
  3. What was the probability that at least one of the first six would not convert?
  4. Optional, if time allows. Timur’s manager says: “Six orders in a row! This channel suddenly sells much better, double the budget!” What would you look at before you agree or disagree?
NoteSolution

  1. No. Under the model the clicks are mutually independent, so by Definition 2.3 the conditional probability that click 7 converts, given that clicks 1 to 6 did, is still \(0.30\). It is Example 2.4(c) again, with clicks in place of coin tosses.

  2. By Definition 2.3, \(0.3^6=0.000729\), about 7 in 10,000.

  3. The complement of (b): \(1-0.000729=0.999271\). Read the question carefully: “at least one does not convert” is not “none converts”. The second would be \(0.7^6\approx0.118\).

  4. Part (b) is the interesting number. Six conversions in a row has probability about 0.0007 if the model is right, which is rare enough to make you doubt the model. Something may have changed. The days before 8 March are the busiest week of the year for flowers, so people who click on a flower ad that week probably buy much more often than 30%. A tracking bug that counts one order six times is another candidate. If the rate itself has gone up, the manager may be right, though the reason is the season and not the streak: the 30% from ordinary months does not apply this week. Problem 9*(d) shows how Bayes’ theorem updates a belief like this, one observation at a time.

The simulation below agrees with (a) and (b), up to random variation. Only about 1,500 of the two million simulated Mondays have a streak, so the last line rests on far fewer cases than the first and is the least precise.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
p_convert, n_mondays = 0.30, 2_000_000
clicks = rng.random((n_mondays, 7)) < p_convert   # True = order
streak = clicks[:, :6].all(axis=1)                # first six all converted
print(f"P(first six convert) = {streak.mean():.5f}, formula = {p_convert**6:.5f}")
print(f"Mondays with a streak: {streak.sum()}")
print(f"P(7th converts | streak) = {clicks[streak, 6].mean():.3f}")
P(first six convert) = 0.00075, formula = 0.00073
Mondays with a streak: 1490
P(7th converts | streak) = 0.299

Problem 6

Calls to the support line of an online electronics shop go to three call centres: 50% to Kazan, 30% to Ufa and 20% to Izhevsk. The Izhevsk centre opened in the spring, and most of its staff are still on probation, the trial months of a new job. A call ends with a written complaint in 1% of cases in Kazan, 2% in Ufa and 4% in Izhevsk.

What share of all calls ends with a complaint?

NoteSolution

Let \(H_1\), \(H_2\), \(H_3\) be “the call went to Kazan”, “to Ufa”, “to Izhevsk”. Every call goes to exactly one centre, so these three events form a partition. Let \(K\) be “the call ends with a complaint”. By Equation 2.2, \[ \Prob(K)=0.01\cdot0.50+0.02\cdot0.30+0.04\cdot0.20=0.005+0.006+0.008=0.019 , \] so 1.9% of calls. In counts: of 10,000 calls, 5000 go to Kazan and 50 of them end with a complaint, 3000 go to Ufa with 60 complaints, and 2000 go to Izhevsk with 80. That is 190 complaints in 10,000 calls.

A quick check: by Theorem 2.4 the answer is a weighted average of 1%, 2% and 4%, an average in which each rate counts as much as its centre’s share of calls. So anything outside the range from 1% to 4% would be wrong.

import numpy as np

share = np.array([0.50, 0.30, 0.20])              # Kazan, Ufa, Izhevsk
p_complaint_given = np.array([0.01, 0.02, 0.04])
p_complaint = np.sum(share * p_complaint_given)
print(f"P(complaint) = {p_complaint:.3f}")
kazan, ufa, izhevsk = np.round(10_000 * share * p_complaint_given).astype(int)
print(f"Complaints per 10,000 calls: Kazan {kazan}, Ufa {ufa}, Izhevsk {izhevsk}")
P(complaint) = 0.019
Complaints per 10,000 calls: Kazan 50, Ufa 60, Izhevsk 80

Problem 7

Continue Problem 6. A written complaint reaches head office, and it does not say which centre took the call. Aigerim, the quality manager, will listen to the recordings of one centre first.

  1. Find the probability that the call went to each of the three centres. Which centre should Aigerim start with?
  2. In (a), Izhevsk takes only 20% of the calls, yet it is the most likely source. Explain why in one sentence.
  3. Next year the shop sends 70% of the calls to Kazan, 20% to Ufa and 10% to Izhevsk. The complaint rates stay the same. Answer (a) again.
NoteSolution

  1. By Equation 2.3, with the denominator \(\Prob(K)=0.019\) from Problem 6, \[ \Prob(H_1\mid K)=\frac{0.005}{0.019}=\frac{5}{19}\approx0.263,\quad \Prob(H_2\mid K)=\frac{0.006}{0.019}=\frac{6}{19}\approx0.316,\quad \Prob(H_3\mid K)=\frac{0.008}{0.019}=\frac{8}{19}\approx0.421 . \] The counts from Problem 6 say the same: of 190 complaints, 50, 60 and 80 came from the three centres. The three answers add up to 1, which is a free check. Aigerim should start with Izhevsk.

  2. Its complaint rate is four times that of Kazan, and that beats its share of calls being 2.5 times smaller: \(0.20\cdot0.04=0.008\) against \(0.50\cdot0.01=0.005\).

  3. New denominator: \(\Prob(K)=0.01\cdot0.70+0.02\cdot0.20+0.04\cdot0.10=0.007+0.004+0.004=0.015\). Then \[ \Prob(H_1\mid K)=\frac{0.007}{0.015}\approx0.467,\qquad \Prob(H_2\mid K)=\Prob(H_3\mid K)=\frac{0.004}{0.015}\approx0.267 . \] Now Kazan is the most likely source, and Aigerim should start there. The complaint rates are the same as before; only the priors moved.

import numpy as np

centres = ["Kazan", "Ufa", "Izhevsk"]
p_complaint_given = np.array([0.01, 0.02, 0.04])
for share in ([0.50, 0.30, 0.20], [0.70, 0.20, 0.10]):
    joint = np.array(share) * p_complaint_given     # P(K and H_i)
    posterior = joint / joint.sum()                 # Bayes: divide by P(K)
    print(f"Shares of calls {share}, P(complaint) = {joint.sum():.3f}:")
    for centre, p in zip(centres, posterior):
        print(f"  P(call went to {centre} | complaint) = {p:.3f}")
Shares of calls [0.5, 0.3, 0.2], P(complaint) = 0.019:
  P(call went to Kazan | complaint) = 0.263
  P(call went to Ufa | complaint) = 0.316
  P(call went to Izhevsk | complaint) = 0.421
Shares of calls [0.7, 0.2, 0.1], P(complaint) = 0.015:
  P(call went to Kazan | complaint) = 0.467
  P(call went to Ufa | complaint) = 0.267
  P(call went to Izhevsk | complaint) = 0.267

Problem 8

At a consumer-loan bank in Krasnoyarsk, 5% of clients default, that is, do not repay. Diego from the credit-risk team is testing a scoring model. On old clients, the model flagged 80% of those who later defaulted and 10% of those who repaid.

  1. Imagine 10,000 applicants. Fill in a table with rows “default” and “repay”, columns “flagged” and “not flagged”, and totals.
  2. A sales manager reads the test report and says: “The model catches 80% of defaulters, so a flagged client defaults with probability 0.8.” Find the correct probability with Bayes’ theorem and check it in your table.
  3. What share of the clients who are not flagged default?
  4. In a recession the default rate rises to 10%, and the model flags defaulters and good clients at the same rates as before. Find the probability that a flagged client defaults now.
NoteSolution

Let \(D\) be “the client defaults” and \(F\) “the model flags the client”. We know \(\Prob(D)=0.05\), \(\Prob(F\mid D)=0.80\) and \(\Prob(F\mid D^c)=0.10\).

  1. 500 of the 10,000 default and 9500 repay. The model flags 80% of the 500, which is 400, and 10% of the 9500, which is 950.
Flagged Not flagged Total
Default 400 100 500
Repay 950 8,550 9,500
Total 1,350 8,650 10,000

  1. The manager has swapped \(\Prob(F\mid D)\) and \(\Prob(D\mid F)\), the mistake of Example 2.1 and Example 2.8. By Equation 2.3 with the partition \(D\), \(D^c\), \[ \Prob(D\mid F)=\frac{0.80\cdot0.05}{0.80\cdot0.05+0.10\cdot0.95}=\frac{0.040}{0.040+0.095}=\frac{0.040}{0.135}\approx0.296 . \] In the table: 400 of the 1,350 flagged clients default, and \(400/1350\approx0.296\). So of 100 flagged loans, about 30 default and about 70 would have been repaid. The flag still means a lot: it raises the chance of default from 5% to about 30%, six times higher. It is just far from 80%, because good clients are 19 times as many as defaulters and 10% of a big group is a lot of people.

  2. \(\Prob(D\mid F^c)=100/8650\approx0.012\). A client who is not flagged is about four times safer than an average client.

  3. Same formula, new prior: \[ \Prob(D\mid F)=\frac{0.80\cdot0.10}{0.80\cdot0.10+0.10\cdot0.90}=\frac{0.08}{0.17}\approx0.471 . \] The model has not changed at all, yet a flag now means almost a 50% chance of default.

n_clients = 10_000
for p_default in (0.05, 0.10):
    n_default = n_clients * p_default
    flagged_default = 0.80 * n_default
    flagged_repay = 0.10 * (n_clients - n_default)
    p_default_given_flag = flagged_default / (flagged_default + flagged_repay)
    print(f"default rate {p_default:.0%}: flagged {flagged_default:.0f} default "
          f"+ {flagged_repay:.0f} repay, P(D | F) = {p_default_given_flag:.3f}")
default rate 5%: flagged 400 default + 950 repay, P(D | F) = 0.296
default rate 10%: flagged 800 default + 900 repay, P(D | F) = 0.471

Problem 9*

This problem goes beyond Lecture 2 and is not examinable. It uses Bayes’ theorem several times in a row, and the new idea is simple: after each piece of evidence, the posterior becomes the prior for the next one.

  1. Diego’s bank from Problem 8 (default rate 5%) adds a second check, a report from a credit bureau. The report comes out “bad” for 70% of the clients who will default and for 20% of the clients who will repay. Assume that the model and the bureau report are conditionally independent given the type of client. In plain words: among defaulters, a bad report is no more and no less likely for flagged clients than for unflagged ones, and the same holds among clients who repay. The model looks at the application form and the bureau at old loans, so once you know what kind of client it is, one check tells you nothing new about the other.

An applicant is flagged by the model. Take the answer of Problem 8(b) as the new prior. What is the probability of default if the bureau report is bad? If it is clean?

  1. Check the “bad report” answer in one step. Find the probability that a defaulter is both flagged and has a bad report, the same for a client who repays, and apply Bayes’ theorem once with the prior 0.05.

  2. Now the money. Every client borrows 300,000 rubles. A default costs the bank 200,000 rubles (it gets part of the money back), and a repaid loan earns 30,000 rubles of interest. The bank compares two rules with lending to everyone:

  • Rule 1: reject every applicant the model flags.
  • Rule 2: for a flagged applicant, also get the bureau report, and reject only if it is bad.

For 10,000 applicants, how much loss does each rule avoid, how much interest does it give up, and what is the difference? Which rule is better? Ignore the price of the bureau reports.

  1. Lecture 2 left one case open: a firm rejected by five banks in a row (Independence). Suppose that 20% of the small firms that apply for loans are poor payers and 80% are ordinary. A bank rejects a poor payer with probability 0.90 and an ordinary firm with probability 0.65, so overall it rejects \(0.2\cdot0.90+0.8\cdot0.65=0.70\) of applications, like the loan officer in the lecture. Given the type of firm, different banks decide conditionally independently. One firm is rejected by bank after bank. Find the probability that it is a poor payer after each of the first five rejections. Then find the probability that a sixth bank also rejects it.

  2. Is the conditional independence in (d) believable? One or two sentences.

NoteSolution

  1. The prior is now \(\Prob(D\mid F)=0.04/0.135=8/27\approx0.296\), and \(\Prob(D^c\mid F)=19/27\). Let \(R\) be “the report is bad”. Conditional independence says that \(\Prob(R\mid D\cap F)=\Prob(R\mid D)=0.70\) and \(\Prob(R\mid D^c\cap F)=\Prob(R\mid D^c)=0.20\), so Equation 2.3 is used exactly as before, only with the new prior: \[ \Prob(D\mid F\cap R)=\frac{0.70\cdot\frac{8}{27}}{0.70\cdot\frac{8}{27}+0.20\cdot\frac{19}{27}}=\frac{5.6}{5.6+3.8}=\frac{28}{47}\approx0.596 . \] A clean report has probability 0.30 for defaulters and 0.80 for good clients: \[ \Prob(D\mid F\cap R^c)=\frac{0.30\cdot\frac{8}{27}}{0.30\cdot\frac{8}{27}+0.80\cdot\frac{19}{27}}=\frac{2.4}{2.4+15.2}=\frac{3}{22}\approx0.136 . \] Both signals together take the applicant from 5% to about 60%. A flag with a clean report leaves about 14%, still well above the 5% we started from.

  2. By conditional independence, \(\Prob(F\cap R\mid D)=0.80\cdot0.70=0.56\) and \(\Prob(F\cap R\mid D^c)=0.10\cdot0.20=0.02\). Then \[ \Prob(D\mid F\cap R)=\frac{0.56\cdot0.05}{0.56\cdot0.05+0.02\cdot0.95}=\frac{0.028}{0.028+0.019}=\frac{28}{47}, \] the same as updating twice. The order of the two checks does not matter either.

The flag and the bad report are not independent events. \(\Prob(F)=0.135\) and \(\Prob(R)=0.70\cdot0.05+0.20\cdot0.95=0.225\), whose product is about 0.030, while \(\Prob(F\cap R)=0.028+0.019=0.047\). Both checks react to the same hidden thing, the type of client. Independence given the type and independence are different statements.

The rule avoids \(280\cdot200{,}000=56{,}000{,}000\) rubles and gives up \(190\cdot30{,}000=5{,}700{,}000\). The net gain is \(50{,}300{,}000\) rubles.
