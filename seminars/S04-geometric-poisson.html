Seminar 4: the geometric and Poisson distributions

PDF · Solutions appear here after the seminar.

\[ \newcommand{\E}{\mathbb{E}} \newcommand{\Var}{\operatorname{Var}} \newcommand{\Cov}{\operatorname{Cov}} \newcommand{\Corr}{\operatorname{Corr}} \newcommand{\Prob}{\mathbb{P}} \newcommand{\R}{\mathbb{R}} \]

Material from Lecture 4 (4  Counting events and measuring time), Sections 4.1, 4.3 and 4.4: the geometric distribution, the Poisson distribution and the Poisson approximation to the binomial. The firms and people on this sheet are made up, and so are their numbers. Start every problem the way the lecture did: write “\(X\) = …” in words, ask what is fixed in advance, and only then name the distribution (Table 4.1). The starred Problem 8* is a longer case for a strong group or to take home. You will need the \(e^x\) key on your calculator (often Shift + ln).

Problem 1

Short drill.

Fatima works at the reception of a hotel in Yaroslavl. At check-in she offers every guest an upgrade to a room with a view of the Volga for 1,500 rubles a night. Each guest says yes with probability 0.15, independently of the others. (Most say they will think about it and never come back to the desk.) Let \(X\) be the number of the guest, counting from the start of her shift, who gives Fatima her first yes.

  1. Which values can \(X\) take? Write \(X\sim\dots\) with the parameter.
  2. Find \(\Prob(X=3)\). Write the path of yes and no answers first.
  3. Find the probability that Fatima needs more than 5 guests to get her first yes.
  4. How many guests does she need on average to get the first yes?

Problem 2

Short drill.

The support line of a car-sharing service in Krasnoyarsk gets on average 5 calls per hour on weekday evenings. The calls come one at a time and independently of each other. Most of them are about a car whose doors will not open. Let \(X\) be the number of calls between 19:00 and 20:00 on one evening.

  1. Write \(X\sim\dots\) with the parameter. Find the probability of exactly 1 call in that hour.
  2. Find the probability of at least 2 calls in that hour.
  3. Let \(Y\) be the number of calls between 19:00 and 19:30. Write \(Y\sim\dots\) and find the probability of no call in those 30 minutes.

Problem 3

Find the mistake.

The problem was:

A factory in Zelenograd makes phone chargers. Each charger is defective with probability 0.05, independently of the others. An inspector tests chargers from the line one at a time until she finds the first defective one. How many chargers does she test on average, the defective one included?

Kirill wrote this solution.

Let \(X\) be the number of chargers tested up to and including the first defective one. Then \(X\) is geometric.

Its PMF is \(\Prob(X=k)=(1-p)^k\) for \(k=1,2,3,\dots\)

The mean of the geometric distribution is \(p\), so \(\E[X]=0.05\).

That can’t be right, it’s less than one charger. A charger is good with probability 0.95, so I take \(p=0.95\) instead: \(\E[X]=1/0.95\approx1.05\), which rounds to 1.

Answer: about 1 charger. This makes sense, because \(X=1\) is the most likely value.

  1. Kirill’s first line is right. What is the “success” here, and what is \(p\)?
  2. Test the PMF line with \(k=1\). What should \(\Prob(X=1)\) be, and what does Kirill’s formula give?
  3. Find the two mistakes in his expected value.
  4. Give the correct answer, one number. Is the last sentence of his answer a good argument?

Problem 4

Main problem.

An online pizzeria in Petrozavodsk gets on average 3 orders per 5 minutes on Friday evenings. The orders come one at a time and independently of each other. The kitchen has two ovens, and the owner wants to know how busy a typical 5 minutes is. Let \(X\) be the number of orders in one 5-minute stretch on a Friday evening, so \(X\sim\operatorname{Pois}(3)\).

  1. Find the probability that the pizzeria gets from 1 to 4 orders in 5 minutes, both ends included. First list the values of \(X\) that are in the event.
  2. True or false? Give the reason in one line each.
  1. The mean number of orders in 15 minutes is 9.
  2. The variance of the number of orders in 5 minutes is 9.
  3. The probability of no order in 10 minutes is \(e^{-6}\approx0.0025\).
  4. The probability of no order in 10 minutes is \(2e^{-3}\approx0.100\), twice the probability of no order in 5 minutes.

Problem 5

Approximation.

An online bookshop in Krasnodar sends 1,000 parcels in a week. Each parcel is lost by the courier with probability 0.003, independently of the others. Let \(X\) be the number of lost parcels in a week.

  1. Which distribution does \(X\) have exactly? Check the rule of thumb from Lecture 4 and name the distribution that approximates it.
  2. Find, approximately, the probability that no parcel is lost in a week, and the probability that at most 2 parcels are lost.
  3. The shop tries a cheaper courier firm, which loses each parcel with probability 0.03, ten times as often. With 1,000 parcels a week, is the rule of thumb still met?

Problem 6

Main problem.

Yegor drives a bakery’s delivery van in Yekaterinburg. He leaves at 5:40, before the shops open, and his route crosses many traffic lights. Every light runs the same 60-second cycle, and then the cycle starts again:

green yellow red yellow
42 s 3 s 12 s 3 s

Yegor stops if he reaches a light during the yellow that comes after green, or during red. During the yellow that comes after red he drives on. The moment at which he reaches a light is equally likely to be any moment of its 60-second cycle, and different lights are independent. Let \(X\) be the number of the light (first, second, …) at which Yegor stops for the first time.

  1. Find the probability \(p\) that Yegor stops at one particular light.
  2. Name the distribution of \(X\). Find \(\E[X]\), \(\Var(X)\) and \(\operatorname{SD}(X)\).
  3. Yegor’s manager asks: “On average, how many lights does he get through before his first stop?” Find this number. Does the variance change if we count the lights he gets through instead of \(X\)?

Problem 7

A decision.

Kamila runs a car-rental desk at Mineralnye Vody airport. Most cars are booked online, but she also keeps some cars for walk-in customers, who come to the desk without a booking. Walk-in requests come one at a time, independently, on average 4 per day, and each needs one car. Every car kept for walk-ins costs the desk parking and insurance whether it is rented or not, so Kamila does not want to keep more than she needs. Let \(X\) be the number of walk-in requests on one day.

The table gives \(\Prob(X=k)\), rounded to four decimals, with one value missing:

\(k\) \(\Prob(X=k)\)
0 0.0183
1 0.0733
2 0.1465
3 0.1954
4 ?
5 0.1563
6 0.1042
7 0.0595
8 0.0298
9 0.0132
  1. Name the distribution of \(X\) with its parameter. Find the missing value \(\Prob(X=4)\).
  2. Add a column \(\Prob(X\le k)\) for \(k=0,1,\dots,9\), adding the probabilities from the top.
  3. What is the smallest number of cars Kamila should keep so that, with probability at least 0.95, every walk-in customer on a day gets a car?

Problem 8*

Longer case.

A plant in Lipetsk makes ceramic floor tiles in batches of 2,000 tiles. Vera, the quality engineer, checks every batch before it goes to the warehouse. The number of critical defects she finds in one batch (a crack through a tile, the wrong glaze colour) has the Poisson distribution with \(\lambda=0.8\). A batch is rejected if it has at least one critical defect. Different batches are independent. The line was started in September and has not settled yet, which is why rejections are so frequent.

  1. Find the probability that a batch is rejected.
  2. On Monday the plant makes two batches. Find the probability that both are rejected.
  3. Let \(Y\) be the number of the batch, counting from Monday’s first, at which the first accepted batch appears. Name the distribution of \(Y\) with its parameter, and find \(\E[Y]\).
